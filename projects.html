Default risk surprises in banks' loan pricing

Job Market Paper, awarded the 2026 ECB Lamfalussy Fellowship.
Check the draft and slides.

In the loan market, similar firms pay different rates, even when they borrow the same loan type from the same bank: do these rate differentials relate to subsequent changes in firms' credit risk? Using two million French loans from 2006–2024 matched to firms' credit ratings assigned by Banque de France, I show that they do: on average, firms whose default probability increases by 1 p.p. one year after loan issuance were charged an otherwise unexplained 0.04 p.p. rate premium at issuance. The premium doubles for younger and smaller firms, strengthens in crises and at longer maturities. I show that higher rates do not themselves cause an increase in credit risk. The positive relationship is therefore consistent with banks possessing information about borrowers beyond that observed by the econometrician; yet, it can also emerge in a market with search frictions, when banks' pricing strategies induce partial separation. I bring these forces together in a search model of loan pricing with asymmetric information in which banks see an imperfect public signal on firms' risk types. Calibrating the model within each rating class, I find that banks are informed and separate borrowers in the safest classes but are uninformed and pool them in the riskiest.

Work in Progress

When Lenders Disagree

I model banks' endogenous information acquisition on firms' types under asymmetric information and imperfect competition. Each bank independently chooses its screening precision and draws a binary signal on the borrower type that is independent of its rival's one, conditional on the borrower type. Disagreement implies that a bank that drew a good signal on a firm may be bidding against a rival that drew a bad one. Equilibrium rates are dispersed, and, when the signal is informative, banks post lower rates when observing a good signal. The value of information depends on whether the rate ranges associated with the two signals share a rate: in this case, an uninformed bank can earn the informed bank's profits by posting a shared rate. Therefore, with a positive and strictly increasing cost of information, only two market configurations survive: banks remain uninformed, or become informed when the two signals' rate ranges are disjoint.

Lender heterogeneity in the pricing of bundled credit

I document new empirical evidence on banks' pricing of bundles. Across 99 French lenders the average gap is −0.05 percentage points, close to zero, but it hides a spread of 1.69 points: nineteen lenders charge a premium, fifteen give a discount, and the rest show no significant gap. Which products a lender sells accounts for 4.9 percent of the variance across lenders, and the rating, size, age and leverage of its borrowers for 0.6 percent. The price gap does not evolve significantly over the bank–firm relationship. Therefore, most of the bundling strategy remains unexplained, and may rather be attributed to lender-specific strategies. This result highlights the strategic choices made by individual banks, and it opens several questions on borrowers' myopia and price discrimination.
